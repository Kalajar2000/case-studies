Here's how I work

I don't start with the product. I start with what the customer already wants, and the words she already uses to say it. My job is to find that, turn it into a reason to buy, and hand my editor something they can build without asking me a single question.

Then everything runs on one loop. Research, idea, brief, test, read the results. Most teams run four of those five and skip the last one. That's the one that makes next week's batch better than this week's, so it's the one I protect.

When the account goes quiet, most people ask the media buyer to fix it. I go backwards instead: read the results harder, then go do more research, then go learn something new. Almost every dry spell I've had came from thin research, not bad buying.

Research — I go find the language, I don't invent it

17 steps. What comes out isn't a document, it's a vocabulary.

How I actually run it The rule
Do one step→ Write down what I found→ Finish it→ Next step

If I do all 17 steps in one sitting and then start writing, I end up with a document nobody opens. One step at a time, and I write while I go.

Careful

⭐ Concept vs angle — get this wrong and nothing else works The big one
Concept — my word, about us

What I'm testing this week. It's a note to my team.

"Us vs them""Before / after""Try it as video"
Angle — her word, about her

The reason she would hand over money.

"Stops the tossing and turning""Gets your energy back"
Quick test: read it out loud. If it doesn't give her a reason to buy, it's not an angle yet. "Us vs them" isn't an angle — us vs them on what? That answer is the angle.
Concept→Angle→Variation→Format→Hook
One angle per ad. The product does ten things. The ad sells one. Try to please everyone and nobody sees themselves in it.

Three ways to make it feel new Try in this order
Way 1
Genuinely new

Something nobody else has. A steel humidifier you can boil, so it never goes mouldy.

Has to be useful
Way 2
True but unsaid

Everyone does it, nobody mentions it. "It's toasted." Every cigarette was toasted. They just said it first and owned it.

+239% in a month
Way 3
Same thing, better name

Chicken stock became bone broth and suddenly it's a health product.

Different beats better. People argue with "better". They get curious about "different". Every idea gets three questions: is it useful, does she care, is it obvious in one line?
What goes in one batch Two ways to load it

Every batch answers one question. The question decides how I fill it.

Way 1 · when I'm lost
Three reasons, one version each

I don't know yet what she responds to. So I put three different reasons to buy in the batch, one execution of each. Whichever one Meta decides to spend on tells me which direction to dig.

Usually imagesFinding the direction
Way 2 · when I know
One reason, three versions

Now I know the reason works. I take that one reason and build three different versions of it. New hook, new visual, new proof, same core reason.

Usually videoGoing deep
3 reasons→find the winner→1 reason, 3 versions→it goes flat→back to 3 reasons
The part people miss: when I'm testing three different reasons, the middle of the video has to work for all three, so I keep it general on purpose. Once I'm testing three versions of one reason, the middle gets specific too. Get that backwards and the ad contradicts itself.
How I check: swap the opening line for a totally different one. Does the rest of the ad still make sense? If yes, that's fine for a three-reason batch, but it's too vague for a deep-dive batch.

Statics — what I change between versions Biggest swing first

When I make version B and C, these are the four things I can change. I go biggest to smallest, and I only change one at a time.

1st
The picture

Whole different visual idea. Different scene, different setup. Biggest swing there is.

2nd
The headline

Same picture, different words on top.

3rd
The benefits

Same picture, different supporting points.

4th
The person in it

Same layout, different woman.

Why the person comes last, since it's technically part of the picture: swapping a 35-year-old for a 58-year-old is the smallest version of a picture change. It's only worth doing once I know the visual idea itself works. If I change the scene and the person and the headline all at once and it wins, I've got a winner and no idea why.
Always three versions. Sometimes the picture was great and the headline was rubbish. Test one version and you'll bin a winner and blame the idea.

⭐ Why my scripts look odd The modular bit
Part 1
3 hooks

Three different openings

Part 2
3 bridges

One to match each hook

Part 3
1 middle

The same for all three

Part 4
1 ending

The same for all three

Video 1 = hook A + bridge A + the middle + the ending
Video 2 = hook B + bridge B + the middle + the ending
Video 3 = hook C + bridge C + the middle + the ending
If the middle changes between the three, and video 2 wins, I've got no idea whether it was the hook or the middle. Keeping it identical is the whole point. That's how I get an answer instead of a result.
If I've written three different middles, I've done it wrong and I start again.
Four things that make an offer land Pick 2–3

Four buttons you can press. I aim for two or three, never all four.

01
New / the only one

02
Easy / anyone can

No effort, no skill, no discipline needed.

03
Safe / it'll work

Low risk. Guarantee, proof, people like her already did it.

04
Big / quick

The result is worth having and it won't take a year.

Pressing all four at once

Four claims, no picture in your head.

Pressing two properly

Test — put it in front of real money

The short version. Full account setup is in the Testing tab.

Where I test and where I scale Two different jobs
Step 1 · Testing
One shared-budget campaign

All the new ads go in one campaign with a shared budget and no interest targeting. Meta decides where the money goes. If it chooses to spend on my ad, that's the signal I wanted.

Step 2 · Scaling
Winners get their own budget

Once an ad proves itself, it moves out into its own ad set with its own fixed budget, so a noisy new batch can't starve it.

Test in the shared-budget campaign→It earns spend→Move it out and give it its own budget
Never the other way round. Forcing budget onto an ad the shared campaign ignored doesn't rescue it. We tried exactly that on 176 ads. One survived. When you force spend you're not creating sales, you're just collecting the ones that were going to happen anyway.
One ad set, one idea Clean data
Never mix. Not images and videos in the same ad set. Three versions of the same idea, nothing else.

I build one ad properly, duplicate it twice, and change only the visual. Same copy, same headline. If three things change at once, the result tells me nothing.

What I check before changing anything Briefed vs shipped

I put the brief and the finished ad side by side and check nine things actually match:

How aware she isThe feelingThe wantWho it's forThe reason to buyThe proofWhat she believesWhere we sitWhy now
This catches a lot. You brief an ad for someone who knows she has the problem, the editor makes one for someone who doesn't, it flops, and you're about to write off a perfectly good angle.
The rule that keeps me honest Guess vs fact
Whatever I think happened is a guess until I rebuild the ad with that one change and it works. Then it's a fact. Until then I say "I think", not "we learned".

And when I build off a winner I never copy the words. I work out what the hook was doing — the feeling it hit and the want it named — and I do that same job with different words.
